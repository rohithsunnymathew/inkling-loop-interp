The cook began by sweating diced onion in olive oil over low heat until it turned soft and translucent, then added minced garlic and a pinch of salt so the garlic would not burn. A spoonful of tomato paste went in next and was stirred until it darkened to a rusty brown, which deepens the flavour of the finished sauce. Canned whole tomatoes were crushed by hand into the pan, along with a bay leaf and a few basil stems, and the sauce simmered gently for forty minutes until it thickened and the oil separated at the edges.
Meanwhile a large pot of water came to a rolling boil and was salted generously, roughly a tablespoon for every few litres, because pasta absorbs seasoning only while it cooks. The spaghetti was cooked until just short of al dente, then transferred with tongs straight into the sauce along with a ladle of the starchy cooking water. Tossing the pasta over high heat for a minute let the starch emulsify the sauce so that it clung to every strand instead of pooling at the bottom of the plate.
Baking bread follows a different rhythm. Flour, water, salt and yeast are mixed into a shaggy dough and left to rest for twenty minutes so the flour can hydrate, a step bakers call autolyse. The dough is then kneaded or folded at intervals until it becomes smooth and elastic, which shows that gluten has developed. After a slow first rise, the dough is divided, shaped into tight rounds and left to proof until it springs back slowly when pressed with a floured finger.
A very hot oven and a covered cast-iron pot produce a crisp crust. Steam trapped under the lid keeps the surface soft during the first part of the bake so the loaf can expand fully, then the lid comes off and the crust browns and blisters. The loaf is done when it sounds hollow when tapped underneath and the internal temperature reaches about ninety-six degrees. Cutting into it before it cools lets steam escape and leaves the crumb gummy.
Roasting vegetables relies on dry heat and space. Carrots, potatoes, parsnips and red onion are cut into even pieces, tossed with oil, salt, pepper and thyme, and spread in a single layer on a heavy tray. Crowding the tray traps moisture and steams the vegetables instead of browning them. Turning them once halfway through the cooking time gives caramelised edges and tender centres, and a squeeze of lemon and a handful of chopped parsley brighten the dish just before serving.
